# The design, and where it comes from

Written for whoever maintains this site next.

The brief asked for Satyajit Ray's art direction. That is a good brief
and an easy one to do badly, so this note records what the research
actually found, what was taken, and the one thing that must not be.

## The rule that matters most

**None of Ray's own work may appear on this site.**

He died on 23 April 1992. Under section 22 of the Indian Copyright Act
1957 artistic works run for the author's life plus sixty years, counted
from the start of the following calendar year. His posters, title
lettering, book jackets, magazine covers and typeface drawings are
therefore protected in India until 31 December 2052. In the EU and the
UK the term is life plus seventy, so 2063. In the United States the
restored term runs to roughly ninety-five years from publication.

There is a specific trap already sitting on Wikimedia Commons. The file
`Poster Mahanagar by Satyajit Ray.jpg` is tagged CC BY 2.0. That licence
is the photographer's, on his photograph of the poster. He had no power
to license Ray's artwork underneath it, and did not.

Several of his posters are also likely to carry a second, independent
copyright belonging to the producer who commissioned them, under
sections 17(b) and (c). A poster can need clearance from both the Ray
estate and the production company printed on the sheet.

The film copyright is a different clock and it misleads people. Indian
cinematograph films run sixty years from publication, so the film
copyright in the 1950s titles has lapsed. That does not release the
lettering. A title card being out of film copyright does not make the
words drawn on it free.

If the committee ever wants to use something of his, the route is the
Society for the Preservation of Satyajit Ray Archives in Kolkata, and
the Ray estate. That is the credit line the British Film Institute
used for its 2013 exhibition and it is the correct door.

**Nothing on this site is sampled from, traced from, or derived from
his artwork.** What follows is a discipline, and a discipline is not
ownable.

## What was taken

**The area ratio, which is the actual style.** On the covers he
designed, the ground occupies between 50 and 69 per cent of the area
and the accent colour between 4 and 8 per cent. Most of the surface is
one colour and very little of it is another. Never more than three or
four colours on one surface, plus the paper. Everything else people
think of as his look follows from that ratio.

**The ground is never white.** Straw, cream, newsprint, kraft. A pure
white page background is the first thing that would make this site stop
looking like anything he made. Our paper is `#f2e7d2`.

**The black is never black.** His sits around `#1c0e16`, a near-black
with a warm violet cast. Ours is warmer and browner, because this is a
paper site about a Bengali festival rather than a film title card, but
there is no `#000000` anywhere in the stylesheet and there should not
be one.

**The hand-drawn title against typeset everything else.** He lettered
the main title of a film by hand and set the smaller credits in type.
The site does the same thing: display sizes carry weight and character,
body text does not compete with them.

**Restraint as a positive act.** The research was blunt about what he
conspicuously does not do, and that list is as useful as anything he
does. No gradients standing in for depth. No drop shadows doing work
that a well-placed edge would do better. No decoration that is not also
structure.

## What was deliberately not taken

The four typefaces he drew, Ray Roman, Ray Bizarre, Daphnis and Holiday
Script, are not available and never have been. Fonts In Use records no
foundry and no distributor for any of them, and the original artwork
was already lost by 1990, when Ray could not find it for his own
exhibition. Only Nemai Ghosh's photographs of the specimen sheets
survive. Anything sold today as "Satyajit Ray's typeface" is not his.

The circular alpana medallion from the *Pather Panchali* poster, the
floating photographic cut-out heads, the fractured title block from
*Pratidwandi*. These are specific, recognisable inventions. Copying
them would be pastiche at best and infringement at worst, and would
look like what it is.

## One thing worth knowing about the man

He is often described as a Brahmo, which is true and misleading. Asked
in his sixties what it meant to him he answered, immediately, "Nothing."
Chandak Sengoopta's 2023 article in *Religions* puts it plainly: Ray was
an atheist for most of his life and found organised religion of every
kind antipathetic.

That is worth holding onto on a website for a religious festival. The
films engage the goddess seriously and sceptically at once. *Devi*
is about a young woman destroyed by being taken for an incarnation.
*Joi Baba Felunath* sets a murder inside a Durga Puja at Benares. The
right inheritance from him here is not reverence. It is attention.

## Sources

The full research is in `research/satyajit-ray.md`, about fifteen
thousand words with eighty-two sources cited inline, and
`research/satyajit-ray.json` carries the measured palette with the
method and its caveats stated for each value.

Two things in there are worth a phone call rather than a search. The
Kolkata research institute TCG CREST has published both complete
*kheror khata* for *Goopy Gyne Bagha Byne*, 941 scanned pages, at
exploreray.org. And a hand-drawn Ray alphabet was framed on the
staircase wall of the old D. J. Keymer office in Kolkata, digitised
once by the designer Nabina Ghosh under Ram Ray's supervision and never
released. It may still be hanging there.
